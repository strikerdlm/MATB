## 1. Introduction and Background

The rapid proliferation of unmanned aircraft systems (UAS) — colloquially known as drones — has prompted national civil aviation authorities (CAAs) worldwide to develop bespoke regulatory frameworks that balance operational freedom with airspace safety. Two jurisdictions of particular analytical interest are Colombia, governed by the *Reglamentos Aeronáuticos de Colombia* (RAC), and the United States, governed by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) under Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations (14 CFR).

Colombia, as a member state of the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) since ratifying the Chicago Convention through Law 12 of 1947, is obligated to align its national aeronautical regulations with ICAO Standards and Recommended Practices (SARPs). Accordingly, the *Unidad Administrativa Especial de Aeronáutica Civil* (UAEAC), commonly known as Aerocivil, has progressively developed its UAS regulatory framework from an initial transitional circular in 2015 to the comprehensive RAC 100 standard adopted in September 2023. This evolution has brought Colombia's UAS framework broadly into alignment with the JARUS three-category model (Open, Specific, Certified) that also underlies EASA regulations in Europe.

The FAA's Part 107 rule, effective August 29, 2016, introduced the first comprehensive small UAS (sUAS) regulatory framework in the United States. It has since been amended to add Remote ID (effective September 2023) and a risk-tiered Operations Over People (OOP) framework. A defining — and internationally distinctive — feature of Part 107 is the explicit rejection of mandatory medical certification for remote pilots, replaced instead by a self-assessment standard under §107.17.

This narrative review is intended to serve researchers, policymakers, aviation medical examiners, and regulatory professionals who seek an exhaustive and cited reference for the two frameworks, with particular focus on the treatment of medical fitness requirements and pilot certification philosophies.

## 2. Colombian Regulatory Framework: RAC 100

### 2.1 Regulatory History and Evolution

The Colombian regulatory trajectory for RPAS/UAS passed through four principal stages:

**Stage 1 — RAC 91 Numeral 4.25.8.2 (Pre-2015):** General aeronautical regulations did not address UAS specifically. UAEAC required case-by-case authorization for all drone operations under general RAC 91 provisions.

**Stage 2 — Circular Reglamentaria 5100-082-002 (Circular 002, July 27, 2015):** The first dedicated RPAS regulatory instrument in Colombia. Issued by the Secretaría de Seguridad Aérea, this circular established transitional rules specifically for *remotely piloted aircraft systems* (RPAS) used for activities other than recreation and sport. It classified RPAS as aircraft pursuant to the RAC framework and required pre-flight authorization requests 15 business days in advance. The Circular recognized RPAS as aircraft consistent with ICAO Circular 328 but applied only to RPAS, not to other UAS types (e.g., autonomous systems). Key limitations: maximum altitude of 500 feet, VLOS operations only, maximum radius of 750 meters from the operator (extendable to 1,500 meters with an observer), and mandatory 40 hours of flight practice for operator certification through an Aerocivil-approved instruction center.

**Stage 3 — Resolución 4201 de 2018 (December 27, 2018, published February 2019):** A major regulatory upgrade that incorporated UAS-specific provisions as Appendix 13 to RAC 91, replacing the 2015 Circular. Critically, this resolution expanded coverage from RPAS only to all UAS (including autonomous systems), classifying them as aircraft under ICAO Circular 328. It established three weight/risk-based operational classes: Class A (up to 25 kg, recreational/hobbyist), Class B (up to 150 kg, commercial), and Class C (>150 kg, experimental only). Class B required formal pilot training, operator registration, liability insurance, and prior UAEAC authorization. Resolución 4201 was administered by the *Dirección de Seguridad Aérea* (DSNA), which had 5 days (12 hours for public-interest matters) to approve or deny authorization requests.

**Stage 4 — RAC 100 (Resolución 01983, September 27, 2023):** The current comprehensive UAS regulatory framework. Published in Diario Oficial No. 52.531, RAC 100 replaced Appendix 13 of RAC 91 in its entirety. RAC 100 is a purpose-built, standalone regulatory chapter within the RAC series, specifically addressing "Operación de Sistemas de Aeronaves No Tripuladas UAS." It introduced the internationally harmonized three-category model (Open/Abierta, Specific/Específica, Certified/Certificada), formal pilot certification by UAEAC issuance, mandatory occupational medical certificates, flight maintenance logbooks, and a structured enforcement regime under RAC 13.

**Amendments to RAC 100:**
- **Enmienda 1 — Resolución 00718 (April 23, 2024):** Aligned RAC 100 with ICAO Annex 19 safety management requirements; modified RAC 219 accordingly.
- **Enmienda 2 — Resolución 03034 (December 27, 2024, effective January 15, 2025):** Extended transition timelines for pilot certification and operator certification; updated Section 100.610 requirements; required full RAC 100 compliance from May 1, 2025 for new certificate applications.

### 2.2 Regulatory Bodies

Under Colombia's dual-authority structure, two governmental entities share jurisdiction over drone operations:

1. **Unidad Administrativa Especial de Aeronáutica Civil (UAEAC / Aerocivil):** The primary civil aviation authority. Aerocivil has exclusive jurisdiction over civilian UAS operations, including: UAS registration, pilot certification (*certificado de idoneidad*), operator certification (*certificado de explotador UAS*), flight authorization (*autorización de vuelo UAS*), airspace management, and enforcement. Contact: grupo.technico@aerocivil.gov.co.

2. **Autoridad Aeronáutica de Aviación de Estado (AAES) / Ministry of National Defense:** Regulates State aviation (military, police, government) UAS operations under the *Reglamento Aeronáutico Colombiano de la Aviación de Estado* (RACAE). When civilian drone operations occur near military installations or in restricted zones, AAES coordination is required. The AAES also has authority to coordinate with police for drone seizures when public security is threatened.

Additional agencies with intersecting jurisdiction include: the Ministry of Agriculture (RAC 137 — agricultural aviation, including agricultural drone spraying), and the Ministry of Environment (applicable to environmental survey operations).

### 2.3 Operational Categories

RAC 100 establishes three categories based on risk level, aligned with the JARUS/EASA international model:

#### Category Abierta (Open Category)
- **Purpose:** Non-commercial, recreational, or non-profit operations.
- **Weight:** UA with maximum gross takeoff weight (PMBO) of up to 25 kg.
- **Operations type:** VLOS only; maximum height of 120 meters (approximately 400 feet) AGL; no prior authorization required.
- **Zones:** Permitted in non-congested areas; minimum 30 meters horizontal distance from persons and buildings in inhabited zones; prohibited within 9 km of airports (6–9 km range permitted below 61 meters height); prohibited within 3 km of heliports.
- **No pre-flight authorization** required from UAEAC.
- **No insurance** required for pure recreational operations.
- **No formal pilot certification** required — operator must understand basic safety rules.
- **Registration:** UAS ≥200 g must be registered; UAS <200 g are exempt.
- **Prohibited:** Operations over crowds, critical infrastructure, military zones, within 1.8 km of the President or foreign dignitaries.

#### Category Específica (Specific Category)
- **Purpose:** Any commercial operation OR any operation exceeding Open Category limits (regardless of intent), including BVLOS, urban overflights, night operations, swarms, cargo transport.
- **Weight:** UA up to 250 kg PMBO.
- **Authorization required:** *Autorización operacional* from UAEAC before each operation.
- **Pilot certification required:** *Certificado de idoneidad para piloto UAS* issued by UAEAC.
- **Operator certification required:** *Certificado de explotador UAS* for companies or commercial entities.
- **Insurance required:** Mandatory liability insurance (póliza de responsabilidad civil extracontractual – RCE) for all specific category or commercial demonstration/instruction operations, regardless of drone weight.
- **Flight/maintenance logbook** (*libro de vuelo y mantenimiento*) required.
- **Applicable to ALL operations** that deviate from open category constraints (e.g., overflight of urban zones, altitudes >120 m, BVLOS, night, drone >25 kg).

**Special operations under Specific Category (requiring adiciones/endorsements):**
| Adición (Endorsement) | Description |
|---|---|
| PBMO >25 kg | Drones from 25 kg to 250 kg PMBO |
| Aspersión | Agricultural spraying/fumigation (also regulated under RAC 137) |
| Dispersión | Seed/material dispersal |
| Enjambre | Coordinated swarm operations (>10 UA) |
| Transporte de carga | Drone delivery operations |
| Vuelo nocturno | Night flight operations |
| BVLOS | Beyond visual line of sight operations |
| Instructor de vuelo UAS | UAS flight instruction |

#### Category Certificada (Certified Category)
- **Purpose:** High-risk operations equivalent to manned aviation: transport of passengers or heavy cargo, operations equivalent to commercial air transport.
- **Status in Colombia:** **Not yet implemented**. Aerocivil has stated this category will be activated once ICAO publishes definitive SARPs for certified UAS operations. Requirements will include airworthiness certificates for the UA, Air Operator Certificate (AOC) for the operator, and aeronautical pilot licenses.

### 2.4 Pilot/Operator Certification Requirements

#### Certificado de Idoneidad para Piloto UAS (Specific Category)

To obtain the *certificado de idoneidad como piloto UAS* from UAEAC, applicants must:

1. **Age and identity:** Be at least **18 years old** with a valid national ID (*cédula de ciudadanía* or *cédula de extranjería*).
2. **Approved training course:** Successfully complete a UAS pilot course at a CIAC (*Centro de Instrucción de Aeronáutica Civil*, authorized under RAC 141/147) or CEAC (*Centro de Entrenamiento de Aeronáutica Civil*) approved by UAEAC. Course content includes: aeronautical regulations, aerodynamics, aeronautical meteorology, navigation, aeronautical communications, safety management systems (SMS), specific UAS knowledge, 20 takeoffs and landings, and emergency procedures. Under the transition rules, prior certificates from RAC 91 Apéndice 13–era instruction centers were valid for specific category operations until April 30, 2025; from May 1, 2025, only full RAC 100-compliant certificates are accepted.
3. **Virtual Learning Object (OVA):** Complete the *Objeto Virtual de Aprendizaje* on unmanned aviation hosted by Aerocivil's CEA.
4. **UAEAC theoretical examination:** Pass with a minimum score of **75%**.
5. **Medical certificate:** Hold a valid *certificado médico ocupacional* with **at least one year of validity** (see §2.5 below).
6. **Application and fees:** Submit *Formato Solicitud de Certificado de Idoneidad de Piloto UAS* with all documentation through Aerocivil's electronic platform; pay applicable administrative fees.

The certificate remains valid only while the occupational medical certificate is current. Renewal of the medical certificate (annually) is required to maintain pilot certification validity.

#### Certificado de Explotador UAS

Commercial operators (natural persons or legal entities providing drone services for compensation) must additionally obtain a *certificado de explotador UAS* by demonstrating: an operations manual, designated *Jefe de Pilotos UAS* (Chief UAS Pilot, who must hold a valid pilot certificate), operational safety management (SMS documentation), and liability insurance. This is mandatory for operations involving image capture for mass media, drone delivery, night operations, and BVLOS operations per transition rules under Resolución 03034/2024.

#### Open Category Operators

No formal certification is required. Operators must: understand basic safety rules; register the UAS if ≥200 g; not operate under the influence of psychoactive substances or alcohol; maintain one operator per UA (no multitasking); ensure basic pre-flight safety checks. Parents/custodians are responsible when minors operate in this category.

### 2.5 Medical Requirements

**This is a key differentiating feature of RAC 100 compared to FAA Part 107.**

Under RAC 100 Sección 100.610, all applicants for the *certificado de idoneidad como piloto UAS* (specific category) must hold a **certificado médico ocupacional** (occupational medical certificate) with at least one year of validity. This must include:

- **Optometric/ophthalmologic assessment:** Visual acuity of 20/20 (corrected or uncorrected) and/or specification of correction required; documentation of corrective lens use if applicable.
- **Auditory and verbal communication capacity:** Sufficient hearing and speech for effective radio communication and crew coordination.
- **Upper limb integrity and movement coordination:** Functional assessment of hands, arms, and motor coordination required for UAS control station operation.

The certificate is issued by an occupational health physician (not necessarily an Aviation Medical Examiner/AME) and must be **renewed annually**. Failure to renew invalidates the *certificado de idoneidad* itself. This is not a Class 1, 2, or 3 aviation medical certificate as defined under RAC 67; it is an occupational health certificate under Colombian labor law medical standards.

**Important distinction:** The *certificado médico ocupacional* is NOT the same as the aviation medical certificates regulated under **RAC 67** (which mirrors ICAO Annex 1 Class 1, 2, and 3 medical standards for commercial airline pilots, private pilots, and air traffic controllers, respectively). RAC 67 Class 1 and Class 2 medical certificates are issued by the *Junta Médica de Aerocivil* or designated AMEs and involve comprehensive aviation-specific physicals including electrocardiography, vision and color discrimination, vestibular testing, and psychological assessment.

**Relationship between RAC 100 and RAC 67:** RAC 100 deliberately chose the occupational medical certificate route rather than requiring RAC 67 aviation medical certificates for UAS pilots, on the rationale that UAS operations present lower risk than manned aviation. The occupational medical certificate is a lower bar — sufficient for the remote pilot workstation environment — while preserving the higher aviation medical standards for manned operations. The JARUS FCL recommendation similarly noted that the "flexibility provided by ICAO Annex 1" regarding medical assessment should be considered "given the particular environment of the RPS and the conduct of RPAS operations" (JARUS FCL Recommendation, Article 4.5.1). Nonetheless, Colombia's requirement for any formal medical assessment at all represents a more stringent approach than the FAA's self-certification model.

**Open Category (no medical certificate required):** Operators in the open category (recreational, ≤25 kg) are not required to hold any formal medical certificate. The prohibition on operating under the influence of psychoactive substances or alcohol applies by general rule.

### 2.7 Registration Requirements

All UAS with PMBO ≥200 g operated in Colombian national territory must be registered in the UAEAC database. Key features:

- **Platform:** Registration through the Aerocivil UAS Colombia electronic platform (https://aerocivil.gov.co) — **no registration fee**.
- **Certificate:** Upon approval, UAEAC issues a *Certificado de Registro del Sistema de Aeronave No Tripulada*.
- **Identification marking:** A permanent label (minimum 2 cm × 4 cm, Arial font size 8) must be affixed to the UA structure containing: UAEAC registration number, owner/operator name or *razón social*, contact email, and Aerocivil contact email. Must be externally visible, high-contrast, and not alter weight/balance/performance.
- **Associated technology:** Cameras, sensors, and other payload equipment associated with the UAS must also be registered.
- **Transition:** UAS registered under the prior RAC 91 Apéndice 13 regime (pre-September 27, 2023) had until June 30, 2024 to update their registration under RAC 100. As of July 1, 2024, unupdated prior registrations are barred from Specific Category operations.
- **Operator registration:** *Explotador UAS* certificates are also registered in the UAEAC database; available for inspection during operations.

### 2.8 Insurance and Liability Requirements

**Specific Category / Commercial Demonstrations / Flight Instruction:** Mandatory *póliza de responsabilidad civil extracontractual* (third-party liability insurance policy) covering damages to persons and property on the surface. The policy must be in force before each operation and is a prerequisite for the *autorización de vuelo UAS*.

**Open Category (recreational):** No insurance required, though operators retain civil, criminal, and police liability for damages caused.

**Enforcement:** Aerocivil may inspect insurance documentation on-site; failure to maintain required insurance can result in denial or revocation of authorization.

### 2.9 Airspace Restrictions

**General rules (all categories):**
- Manned aviation has **priority** at all times; UAS must yield to all manned aircraft.
- Must comply with RAC 91 general flight rules.
- No operations from moving vehicles, vessels, or manned aircraft.
- No arming of drones with weapons; no transport of prohibited substances.
- No operations in Special Use Airspace (restricted, prohibited, or danger areas) without AAES/UAEAC coordination.

**Specific proximity restrictions:**
| Zone | Restriction |
|---|---|
| Airports/aerodromes | Prohibited within 9 km radius; permitted 6–9 km below 61 m height |
| Heliports | Prohibited within 3 km |
| President / VP / foreign heads of state | Prohibited within 1.8 km |
| Military/police bases, prisons | Prohibited within 1 km |
| National borders | Prohibited within 3.6 km |
| Critical infrastructure (dams, power plants, etc.) | Requires coordination |

**Altitude limits:**
- Open Category: Maximum 120 meters (approximately 400 feet) AGL.
- Specific Category: Authorized altitude per the *autorización de vuelo UAS*; up to the airspace class limits.

**Night operations:** Permitted under Specific Category only, with appropriate UA lighting (navigation lights), specific pilot endorsement (*vuelo nocturno adición*), and UAEAC authorization.

**FPV (First-Person View):** Permitted but only with a designated visual observer maintaining direct contact with the UA and communicating in real time with the pilot; treated similarly to EVLOS.

### 2.10 BVLOS Operations

BVLOS (Beyond Visual Line of Sight) operations are the most heavily regulated category and require:

1. **Specific Category classification** (mandatory — always exceeds Open Category limits).
2. **BVLOS adición** on the pilot's *certificado de idoneidad* (requires additional training: 2–8 hours classroom + 4–8 hours flight/simulator).
3. **BVLOS system approval:** The UAS must incorporate a *sistema tecnológico de gestión de vuelo UAS* (UTM/flight management technology system) integrating: C2 (command and control) link, real-time GIS for position/control/separation/tracking, and failure response protocols.
4. **Explotador UAS certification** with approved BVLOS capabilities.
5. **Pre-operational flight tests and procedure validation** as required by UAEAC.
6. **UAEAC authorization** for each operation.
7. **Real-time meteorological monitoring** equipment.

*EVLOS (Extended VLOS):* An intermediate modality using visual observers, permitted up to 3 km from the pilot. Each observer must maintain continuous communication with the remote pilot.

### 2.12 Relationship Between RAC 100 and RAC 67

**RAC 67** is the Colombian medical certification standard for airmen, mirroring ICAO Annex 1 requirements. It governs three classes of aviation medical certificates:
- **Class 1:** Required for commercial airline transport pilots (ATP) and commercial pilots. Comprehensive physical standards including cardiovascular, neurological, visual (color perception, 6/9 corrected), audiometric, and psychological assessment. Issued by UAEAC's *Junta Médica* or designated AMEs.
- **Class 2:** Required for private pilots, flight engineers, and navigators.
- **Class 3:** Required for air traffic controllers.

**RAC 100's Deliberate Departure from RAC 67:** RAC 100 Sección 100.610 requires an *occupational medical certificate* — NOT a Class 1, 2, or 3 aviation medical certificate under RAC 67. This was a deliberate policy choice reflecting the lower risk profile of UAS operations compared to manned aviation:

1. The remote pilot is not aboard the aircraft and therefore cannot be physically incapacitated in the same sense as a manned aircraft pilot.
2. The operational risk profile (low altitude, VLOS primary, no passengers) does not justify the expense and barrier of full aviation medical certification.
3. The occupational medical certificate is a proportionate standard: it assesses visual, auditory, and motor functions relevant to UAS control station operation without the full burden of an aviation AME examination.

This relationship mirrors — but does not exactly replicate — the JARUS FCL recommendation, which suggests a "Medical Certificate Class 3" for certified RPAS operations while explicitly noting that competent authorities "may consider using the flexibility provided by ICAO Annex 1 regarding the medical assessment given the particular environment of the RPS" (JARUS FCL Recommendation, Article 4.5.1).

## 3. FAA 14 CFR Part 107

### 3.1 Regulatory Framework Overview

The FAA's Small UAS Rule (14 CFR Part 107) took effect on August 29, 2016, following a notice-and-comment rulemaking process that generated over 4,500 public comments. The rule applies to small UAS (sUAS) — unmanned aircraft weighing less than **55 pounds** (~24.9 kg) — operated in the National Airspace System (NAS) for **non-recreational commercial purposes**. Recreational operations are separately governed under 49 U.S.C. §44809 (the Special Rule for Model Aircraft / Exception for Limited Recreational Operations).

The rule is organized into four subparts:
- **Subpart A:** Applicability and General Requirements
- **Subpart B:** Operating Rules (§§107.11–107.59)
- **Subpart C:** Remote Pilot Certification (§§107.61–107.79)
- **Subpart D:** Waivers (§107.200–107.205)

**Key regulatory authority:** 49 U.S.C. §§ 106(f), 40103(b), 44701(a), 44703, 45302; Public Law 112-95, §333.

### 3.2 Remote Pilot Certificate Requirements

To operate a small UAS under Part 107, a person must hold — or be under the direct supervision of someone who holds — a **Remote Pilot Certificate with a Small UAS Rating** (Remote Pilot Certificate or RPC).

**Eligibility requirements (§107.61):**
- Minimum age: **16 years old**
- Ability to read, speak, write, and understand English
- Not known or reasonably suspected to pose a security threat (TSA security vetting)
- Demonstrate aeronautical knowledge (via initial or recurrent knowledge testing)

**Obtaining the certificate (§107.63–§107.65):**
Two pathways:

*Pathway 1 (Non-Part 61 holders):*
1. Obtain an FAA Tracking Number (FTN) via IACRA (Integrated Airman Certification and Rating Application) profile creation.
2. Schedule and pass the **Part 107 Aeronautical Knowledge Test** at an FAA-approved knowledge testing center (83-question multiple choice test; fee approximately $175). Topics include: applicable regulations, airspace classification, weather, loading and performance, emergency procedures, airport operations, physiological effects of drugs and alcohol, aeronautical decision-making, crew resource management, and maintenance/inspection.
3. Submit FAA Form 8710-13 via IACRA.
4. Receive a temporary Remote Pilot Certificate upon TSA security clearance (typically within 10 business days).

*Pathway 2 (Existing Part 61 Certificate Holders with current flight review):*
- Must hold a Part 61 pilot certificate and have completed a flight review within the previous 24 calendar months.
- Complete FAA online Part 107 small UAS training course (ALC-515, no cost).
- No separate knowledge test required.
- Immediately receive a temporary RPC upon application.

**Recency requirements:** Certificate holders must complete an online recurrent training course every **24 calendar months** to maintain aeronautical knowledge currency:
- Part 107 holders (only): ALC-677 (Part 107 Small UAS Recurrent, no cost)
- Part 107 + Part 61 (with current flight review): ALC-515

Unlike Colombia, **no practical/flight demonstration is required** to obtain the Remote Pilot Certificate. The FAA has acknowledged this as a gap: there is no federal requirement to demonstrate actual ability to fly a drone. FAA Advisory Circular 107-2B (2021) provides guidance but does not mandate practical testing.

**Certificate validity:** The Remote Pilot Certificate itself does not expire. Only recurrent training every 24 months is required.

### 3.3 Medical Self-Certification (§107.17)

**Section 107.17 — Medical Condition:**
*"No person may manipulate the flight controls of a small unmanned aircraft system or act as a remote pilot in command, visual observer, or direct participant in the operation of the small unmanned aircraft if he or she knows or has reason to know that he or she has a physical or mental condition that would interfere with the safe operation of the small unmanned aircraft system."*

This is the entirety of the medical standard for Part 107 operations. **There is no requirement for any medical examination, medical certificate, or self-certification statement.** The provision is entirely self-assessed: each operator is individually responsible for knowing their own physical and mental limitations before each flight.

**FAA's Rationale for Not Requiring Medical Certification:**

In the preamble to the final Part 107 rule (81 Fed. Reg. 42064, June 28, 2016), the FAA addressed approximately 115 public comments on the medical certification question and articulated the following rationale for rejecting mandatory medical certificates:

1. **Lower risk than manned operations:** "Small UAS operations present a lower risk than manned operations to manned aircraft and non-participating people on the ground, especially because the operations do not involve any human beings onboard the aircraft who could be injured in the event of an accident."

2. **Operational limitations mitigate incapacitation risks:** The operational constraints of Part 107 — speed limit of 87 knots, prohibition on operations over non-participating persons, visual line-of-sight requirement — create a "confined area of operation" that minimizes the consequences of remote pilot incapacitation compared to manned aviation.

3. **Loss of control parity:** "The risks associated with pilot incapacitation are similar to the risks associated with loss of positive control." Those risks are already mitigated by: (a) preflight inspection of control links; (b) 87-knot speed limit; (c) prohibition on flight over non-participants.

4. **No list of disqualifying conditions:** The FAA explicitly declined to enumerate disqualifying medical conditions because "there are a wide range of small UAS operations that could be affected differently by different medical conditions." For example, a pilot who lacks manual dexterity might still safely operate a voice-controlled UAS.

5. **Individual responsibility:** The operator "is responsible for knowing his or her physical and mental limitations and evaluating whether those limitations would allow him or her to safely participate in the specific small UAS operation that he or she is considering." If uncertain, the operator should consult a physician.

6. **Historical precedent:** The FAA noted that glider and balloon pilots have historically been allowed to exercise privileges without a medical certificate or driver's license, "and this practice has resulted in no adverse effects on the NAS."

7. **Vision standard rejected:** The FAA considered but rejected a prescriptive vision examination, finding that the VLOS requirement itself is a more functionally appropriate safety mechanism.

**AC 107-2B Examples of Disqualifying Conditions (Guidance, Not Regulatory):**
While not regulatory, Advisory Circular 107-2B provides illustrative examples of conditions that would likely violate §107.17:
- Temporary or permanent loss of dexterity needed to operate the control station.
- Inability to maintain "see and avoid" vigilance due to blurred vision.
- Inability to maintain situational awareness due to illness or medications with driving/machinery warnings.
- Debilitating pain (e.g., migraine, severe body ache) preventing operational duties.
- A hearing/speaking impairment that prevents effective communication with visual observer (unless alternative communication is implemented).

**Relationship to §61.53:** The preamble analogized §107.17 to 14 CFR §61.53, which requires manned aircraft pilots to abstain from acting as pilot-in-command during periods of known medical deficiency — regardless of whether they hold a medical certificate. This principle of self-responsibility pre-exists Part 107 in the manned aviation context.

**No removed self-certification provision:** In the Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM), the FAA had proposed requiring applicants to submit a certified statement attesting to physical and mental condition at time of application. This was removed from the final rule because a person's medical condition "at the time he or she submits his or her application for a remote pilot certificate may change prior to operation of the small UAS."

### 3.4 Operational Limitations

Under 14 CFR §§107.25–107.59, Part 107 operations must comply with the following default limitations (most waivable):

| Parameter | Default Limitation |
|---|---|
| Maximum weight | <55 lbs (~24.9 kg) including everything on board |
| Maximum altitude | 400 feet AGL (or within 400 ft of a structure) |
| Maximum speed | 100 mph (87 knots) |
| Visibility | Minimum 3 statue miles from control station |
| Distance from clouds | 500 feet below; 2,000 feet horizontal |
| Operations over people | Prohibited (unless OOP categories apply or waiver) |
| Operations from moving vehicle | Prohibited (except over sparsely populated areas, no property transport for compensation) |
| Daylight operations | Civil twilight (30 min before sunrise to 30 min after sunset) with anti-collision lighting |
| Visual line of sight | Required; unaided (glasses/contacts permitted) |
| Multiple UA | Prohibited (one at a time; waivable) |
| Careless or reckless operations | Prohibited |
| Yielding to manned aircraft | Always required |
| Hazardous materials | Carriage regulated separately |

**Airspace authorization:** Operations in controlled airspace (Classes B, C, D, and the surface area of Class E) require prior authorization via the **LAANC** (Low Altitude Authorization and Notification Capability) system or via **DroneZone** manual authorization. LAANC provides near-real-time authorizations at approved airports and is available to Part 107 pilots through more than 700 facilities. Class G (uncontrolled) airspace requires no authorization.

**Reporting requirements:** Accidents involving serious injury, loss of consciousness, or property damage exceeding $500 must be reported to the FAA within 10 days.

### 3.5 Registration Requirements

All sUAS weighing more than **0.55 lbs (250 g)** and less than 55 lbs (24.9 kg) must be registered before first operation. Key features:
- **Platform:** FAA DroneZone (https://faadronezone.faa.gov)
- **Fee:** $5 per drone (3-year registration)
- **Certificate:** Must be accessible (physical or electronic) during operations
- **Marking:** Registration number must be marked on the drone (engraved, permanent marker, or label)
- **Remote ID integration:** Registration must now include the Remote ID serial number for Standard Remote ID drones or broadcast modules.

### 3.6 Waivers and Authorizations

Part 107 provides a formal waiver mechanism under §107.200 for 10 specific operational limitations that can be waived if the applicant demonstrates equivalent safety through an alternative means:

| Waivable Section | Subject |
|---|---|
| §107.25 | Operation from a moving aircraft or vehicle in populated areas |
| §107.29 | Night operations without anti-collision lighting |
| §107.31 | Visual Line of Sight (BVLOS) |
| §107.33 | Visual Observer requirements |
| §107.35 | Multiple simultaneous small UAS |
| §107.39 | Operations over human beings (not meeting OOP categories) |
| §107.41 | Operations in controlled airspace (alternative to LAANC) |
| §107.51 | Altitude, speed, visibility, and cloud clearance limitations |
| §107.53 | Daylight operations |
| §107.57 | Operation from moving vehicle over populated area |

**BVLOS waiver process:** FAA evaluates BVLOS waiver applications with the highest scrutiny, requiring detailed ConOps (Concept of Operations), hazard analysis, and demonstration of safety equivalence. The FAA has been expanding BVLOS access through the BEYOND program, drone corridors, and recent regulatory proposals that would create a risk-based authorization framework.

### 3.7 Remote ID (2023)

The **Remote Identification Rule** (14 CFR Part 89) took effect on April 21, 2021, with enforcement beginning **March 16, 2024** (extended from September 16, 2023). Requirements:

- All registered drones must either: (a) broadcast **Standard Remote ID** (from an integrated system), or (b) use a **Remote ID Broadcast Module** attached to the drone.
- Broadcast data includes: drone's unique identifier, location, altitude, velocity, control station location, timestamp, and emergency status.
- Applies to all Part 107 commercial operations and registered recreational drones.
- **Exception:** Drones may operate without Remote ID only in an FAA-Recognized Identification Area (FRIA) or with a Remote ID Letter of Authorization (LOA).
- **Weight threshold:** No specific weight threshold stated in Part 89; applies to all drones required to register (>0.55 lbs / 250 g).

### 3.8 Operations Over People (OOP)

Effective March 2021 (86 Fed. Reg. 4314), the FAA created a risk-tiered framework permitting operations over people under defined conditions:

| Category | Description | Drone Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Category 1 | ≤0.55 lbs (250 g); minimal safety risk | No kinetic energy limits; no design requirement |
| Category 2 | Transfer <11 ft-lbs kinetic energy; exposed rotating parts shielded | Declaration of Compliance (DOC) to FAA |
| Category 3 | Transfer <25 ft-lbs kinetic energy | DOC; restricted to non-congested areas (unless sustained flight over assemblies under waiver) |
| Category 4 | FAA Airworthiness Certificate; operating limitations; Remote ID | FAA certificate; operator manual requirement |

**Sustained flight over open-air assemblies** remains prohibited unless under Category 4 or a §107.39 waiver.

### 3.9 Knowledge Test Requirements

The **Part 107 Aeronautical Knowledge Test** (FAA-approved testing centers; test code UAG):
- 60 questions; multiple choice; 120-minute time limit; $175 fee at testing centers
- Passing score: **70%** (42 of 60 correct)
- **No practical flight test required**
- Topics: applicable FAA regulations, national airspace system, weather effects, loading/performance, emergency procedures, physiological effects of drugs and alcohol, aeronautical decision-making, maintenance/inspection

**Recurrent knowledge currency:** Every 24 months via free online FAA course. Note that the 2022 update replaced the recurrent knowledge test with the online course requirement.

## 4. International Framework

### 4.1 ICAO Circular 328 (Unmanned Aircraft Systems, 2011)

ICAO Circular 328 (AN/190), published December 2011, was the foundational international guidance document that established the conceptual framework for UAS integration into non-segregated airspace. Key positions:

- UAS are aircraft and therefore subject to the Convention on International Civil Aviation (Chicago Convention) and applicable SARPs.
- Contracting States must ensure that flights of pilotless aircraft in airspace open to civil aviation "be controlled so as to obviate danger to civil aircraft" (Article 8 of the Chicago Convention).
- The circular established the term RPAS (Remotely Piloted Aircraft Systems) and distinguished RPA from fully autonomous UA.
- Recommended that RPAS operations not increase safety risks to manned aircraft.
- Identified the "see-and-avoid" challenge as the central safety problem for RPAS integration.
- Encouraged States to contribute to ICAO SARPs development based on national experience.

Both Colombia and the United States explicitly reference Circular 328 in their regulatory frameworks: Aerocivil cited it in Resoluciones 4201/2018 and 01983/2023; FAA UAS ARC used it as a foundational reference.

### 4.2 ICAO Doc 10019 — Manual on Remotely Piloted Aircraft Systems (RPAS)

Published in 2015 (First Edition), ICAO Doc 10019 (AN/507) provides comprehensive technical guidance on RPAS integration. Key relevant provisions:

- **Scope:** Focused on *certificated RPAS* operating in non-segregated international airspace under Instrument Flight Rules (IFR), primarily targeting Block 2 (2025) and Block 3 (2031) ICAO Aviation System Block Upgrades (ASBUs).
- **Remote Pilot Station (RPS):** Guidance covers the human factors aspects of operating from an RPS, noting that "the absence of a pilot on board also brings the challenge of matching the ability of the pilot to 'see and avoid' and 'remain-well-clear' of other traffic."
- **Personnel licensing:** Doc 10019 recommends that States assess remote pilot licensing requirements on the basis of the operational environment of the RPS, the complexity of the operation, and the applicable airspace. This flexibility is the basis on which both Aerocivil (occupational medical) and FAA (self-certification) have premised their approaches.
- **C2 link:** Extensive guidance on command-and-control link reliability requirements for safe RPAS operation.
- **Current update status:** Doc 10019 is currently being updated by ICAO to reflect the latest RPAS SARPs.

### 4.3 EASA Regulation (EU) 2019/947

The European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA) implemented Regulation (EU) 2019/947 (effective December 31, 2020; fully applicable January 1, 2024) as the primary UAS operational regulation for EU member states plus Switzerland, Norway, Iceland, and Liechtenstein. This regulation is the direct European analogue to both RAC 100 and Part 107, making it a useful third-jurisdiction reference.

**Three-category structure:**

| Category | Description | Alignment with Colombia/FAA |
|---|---|---|
| Open | Low risk; ≤25 kg; VLOS; ≤120 m AGL; no authorization | Same as Colombia Open; roughly equivalent to Part 107 standard operations |
| Specific | Moderate risk; requires risk assessment (SORA/PDRA) or Standard Scenario declaration; BVLOS | Same as Colombia Specific; requires waiver in Part 107 |
| Certified | High risk; equivalent to manned aviation; full certification | Same as Colombia Certified (not yet active) |

**Subcategories (Open):**
- A1: Fly over people (not assemblies); drones <250 g or C0/C1 class
- A2: Fly close to people; ≥30 m distance; C2 class drones
- A3: Fly far from people (≥150 m from residential/commercial/industrial areas); C3/C4 class drones

**Remote pilot competence:** Online theoretical training and examination required for A1/A3 (EU certificate valid 5 years); practical skills assessment additionally required for A2. No medical certificate required. Minimum age: 16 years (no minimum for C0/toy drones).

**Medical requirements:** EASA does **not** require any medical certificate for Open or Specific category operations. Regulation (EU) 2019/947 Article 8 states that pilots "shall not fly when under the influence of psychoactive substances or alcohol, or when unfit due to sickness." This is analogous to FAA §107.17 (self-assessment) and is **less stringent** than Colombia's mandatory occupational medical certificate.

**Remote ID:** Mandatory since January 1, 2024 for most drones ≥250 g and for all specific category operations; technically equivalent to FAA Remote ID. C1–C3 drones sold new after 2024 must include integrated Remote ID.

**SORA (Specific Operations Risk Assessment):** EASA's Specific category primarily uses the JARUS-developed SORA methodology as the basis for operational authorization, providing a structured risk assessment framework that determines required risk mitigations (including personnel competency level) based on ground and air risk matrices.

### 4.4 JARUS (Joint Authorities for Rulemaking on Unmanned Systems) Guidelines

JARUS is a voluntary international organization comprising 73 national aviation authority members (as of 2025) plus EASA, EUROCONTROL, and industry stakeholders. It develops harmonized international recommendations for UAS regulations that member states may adopt or adapt. JARUS is chaired by Italy (Giovanni Di Antonio) with Kazakhstan as Vice-Chair.

**Relevant JARUS documents:**

**JAR Doc 03 — JARUS-FCL Recommendation (Personnel Licensing):**
- Recommends a formal remote pilot licence (RPL or VLOS RPL) for certificated (Category C) RPAS operations.
- Requires a **Medical Certificate Class 3** for RPL applicants (the lowest tier of ICAO Annex 1 aviation medical certification, applied to air traffic controllers in the manned aviation context).
- Explicitly notes that "the competent authority may consider using the flexibility provided by ICAO Annex 1 regarding the medical assessment given the particular environment of the RPS and the conduct of RPAS operations" — providing the regulatory basis for less stringent medical standards.
- Medical certificate issued by "competent authority or designated medical entities."

**JAR Doc 11 — JARUS UAS RPC CAT A AND CAT B (Remote Pilot Competency):**
- Article 5(1): "The remote pilot should be in a physical and mental condition such that they would not endanger the safe operation of the UAS, other aircraft, persons, environment, animals or property."
- Article 5(2): Category B remote pilots **must declare** to the UAS operator that they are medically fit as determined in the applicable SORA.
- No formal medical examination required for Category A or B — **declaration-based** approach for Category B.
- The level of medical fitness assessment is determined by the SORA risk assessment, which may require no formal medical testing for low-risk operations.

**JAR Doc 06 — SORA (Specific Operations Risk Assessment):**
The SORA framework evaluates Operational Safety Objectives (OSOs), including OSO #9, 15, and 22 relating to remote crew training and competency. Medical fitness requirements are embedded within these OSOs based on the operation's Ground Risk Class (GRC) and Air Risk Class (ARC). As of SORA 3.0 (work in progress, 2025), the medical fitness requirements are proportionate to risk level rather than prescriptive.

**JAR Doc 10 — JARUS UAS Operational Categorization:**
States that "for highest risk operations, operator and pilot certificates should be required; the requirements should be comparable to requirements concerning manned aviation." For lower-risk operations, a pilot certificate alone (without the full RAC 67/Part 67 aviation medical rigor) may be appropriate.

### 5.2 Medical Certification: Colombia vs. FAA

This dimension represents the sharpest regulatory divergence between the two frameworks and is of particular relevance for journal narrative review.

**Colombia (RAC 100 — More Stringent):**
- Mandatory formal medical examination for all specific category pilots.
- Administered by an occupational health physician (not an AME under RAC 67).
- Specific functional domains assessed: vision (20/20 corrected/uncorrected), hearing/verbal communication, upper limb integrity and motor coordination.
- Annual renewal required; certificate expiry invalidates pilot certification.
- Rationale: proportionate approach — higher bar than pure self-certification, lower bar than full RAC 67 aviation medical.
- Basis: Risk-appropriate occupational health standard rather than aviation standard, acknowledging the JARUS/ICAO position that RPAS operations occur in an environment different from manned cockpits.

**United States (Part 107 — Less Stringent):**
- **No medical examination whatsoever** required.
- Entirely self-assessed prohibition on operating with known physical or mental conditions that would interfere with safe operation.
- No specific vision, hearing, or motor function standards.
- No annual or periodic medical review.
- Rationale: The risk profile of small UAS (no human aboard, operational constraints) does not justify the access barrier and cost of formal medical certification.
- Grounded in precedent: glider/balloon pilot operations without medical certificates; general self-responsibility principle under §61.53.

**Assessment:**
Colombia's mandatory annual occupational medical certificate is objectively more restrictive for individual pilots. It imposes a genuine, if moderate, barrier to specific category commercial operations. The requirement for visual acuity 20/20 (corrected), auditory fitness, and upper limb coordination assessment adds a functional safety layer absent in Part 107.

However, Colombia's standard is significantly *less* demanding than the ICAO Annex 1 / RAC 67 aviation medical certificates required for manned aircraft pilots. The system thus occupies a deliberate middle ground: imposing a minimum fitness threshold without creating the cost and access burden of full aviation medicine.

The FAA's self-certification approach is justified on theoretical risk-management grounds and has operated since 2016 without documented incidents attributable to undetected pilot medical incapacitation. However, critics (including the 2007 DOT/Volpe Center study, which recommended second-class medical certification for UAS operators) have noted that the absence of any objective medical assessment is a gap in the safety net, particularly for complex or high-risk UAS operations.

**JARUS position:** Aligns neither fully with Colombia nor FAA. JAR Doc 11 (Category A and B) requires only a self-declaration of medical fitness for Category B — effectively the FAA model. JAR Doc 03 (FCL for Category C / Certified) recommends a formal Medical Certificate Class 3 — approaching Colombia's model but at a higher aviation-standard bar.

### 5.3 Risk Management Approaches

| Dimension | Colombia RAC 100 | FAA Part 107 |
|---|---|---|
| **Risk Framework** | Categorical (Open/Specific/Certified) based on risk level | Prescriptive rules with waiver mechanism |
| **SORA Adoption** | Not explicitly adopted (uses authorization process) | Not adopted (waiver-based) |
| **Authorization Model** | Pre-flight authorization required for all specific category operations | Airspace authorization via LAANC/DroneZone; no per-operation authorization required for standard Part 107 |
| **Safety Management** | SMS documentation required for Explotador UAS | Not required by rule |
| **Incident Reporting** | Required for accidents/incidents (Aerocivil) | Required for serious injury/LOC/≥$500 damage within 10 days |
| **Maintenance Records** | Mandatory libro de vuelo y mantenimiento | Not mandated by regulation |

Colombia's categorical/authorization-based model imposes **higher pre-operational compliance burden** (per-flight authorization for specific category) but provides a clear regulatory pathway. The FAA's model provides **more operational flexibility** within defined constraints, with a robust waiver system for non-standard operations.

The EASA/JARUS SORA methodology represents a more sophisticated, risk-proportionate third way that neither Colombia nor the FAA has fully adopted in their sUAS frameworks, though both are influenced by its concepts.

### 5.4 Operational Categories Alignment

| Colombia RAC 100 | EASA (EU) 2019/947 | FAA Part 107 (approximate) |
|---|---|---|
| Abierta (Open) ≤25 kg, recreational | Open A1/A2/A3 ≤25 kg | Standard Part 107 (≤55 lbs, non-waivable limits) |

Note: A key structural difference is that Colombia and EASA use the *category* to determine pilot certification requirements, insurance obligations, and authorization needs, while the FAA uses a single certification standard (Remote Pilot Certificate) with a waiver pathway for exceeding default limits.

### 5.5 Which Jurisdiction Is More Restrictive?

The answer depends on the dimension being evaluated:

**Colombia is more restrictive regarding:**
- **Medical certification:** Mandatory annual occupational health exam vs. FAA self-assessment only.
- **Pilot age:** 18 years minimum (specific category) vs. 16 years (FAA).
- **Practical flight experience:** Required (20 takeoffs/landings at certified training center) vs. none required by FAA.
- **Insurance:** Mandatory for all commercial operations vs. not required by FAA Part 107.
- **Per-operation authorization:** Required for all specific category operations (prior approval from Aerocivil) vs. airspace authorization only (LAANC) for FAA.
- **Operator certification:** *Certificado de Explotador UAS* required for commercial entities vs. no separate FAA operator certificate.
- **Flight maintenance logbooks:** Mandatory under RAC 100 specific category vs. not mandated by Part 107.

**FAA is more restrictive regarding:**
- **Remote ID:** Mandatory for all registered drones since March 2024 vs. no equivalent currently in RAC 100.
- **Weight ceiling for standard rule:** 55 lbs (~24.9 kg) for Part 107; Colombia's open category permits up to 25 kg but specific category goes up to 250 kg (with certification).
- **Knowledge test score:** 70% passing score vs. 75% in Colombia.

**Overall Assessment:** For commercial (specific category) operations, Colombia is meaningfully more restrictive than FAA Part 107, particularly in: medical certification requirements, practical training requirements, per-operation authorization burden, and mandatory insurance. FAA Part 107 provides significantly more operational flexibility for commercial operators who meet the remote pilot certificate standard.

## 6. Key Findings and Observations

1. **Medical certification divergence is substantive:** Colombia's mandatory annual occupational medical certificate for specific category pilots represents a genuine regulatory gap compared to FAA self-certification. The Colombian approach more closely aligns with JARUS FCL recommendations for Category C/Certified operations (Medical Certificate Class 3) than with the JAR Doc 11 Category B standard (self-declaration), placing Colombia at a higher-than-international-minimum bar for commercial UAS operations.

2. **The practical flight test requirement is a Colombia-unique safety feature:** No country in the comparison (FAA, EASA) requires a demonstrated practical flight competency for commercial drone certification. Colombia's requirement of 20 supervised takeoffs/landings in an approved training environment addresses a documented gap in the FAA system (noted in academic literature: Schall et al., 2023) where commercially certified pilots need not have ever flown a drone.

3. **RAC 100's three-category model aligns with ICAO/JARUS/EASA:** Colombia's adoption of the Open/Specific/Certified taxonomy demonstrates greater harmonization with the emerging international framework than the FAA's waiver-based single-rule approach. This positions Colombian operators for easier regulatory recognition abroad.

4. **Remote ID is a current RAC 100 gap:** Colombia lacks an equivalent to FAA Remote ID (Part 89) or EASA Remote ID requirements. This is a notable safety and security gap, particularly as Colombia faces significant challenges with illegal drone use by non-state actors in conflict zones (Drones y Guerras de Quinta Generación, Revista Logos Ciencia y Tecnología, 2025).

5. **BVLOS regulation:** Both jurisdictions treat BVLOS as high-risk and require special authorization, but the technological requirements in RAC 100 (explicit UTM/C2/GIS integration requirement) are more prescriptive than the FAA waiver process, which evaluates proposals on a case-by-case basis.

6. **Insurance gap:** The FAA's non-requirement of liability insurance for commercial Part 107 operations is notable given the personal injury and property damage potential of commercial drone operations. Colombia's mandatory RCE insurance for specific category operations better aligns with ICAO guidance on liability (Warsaw/Montreal Convention analogy) and international best practices.

7. **Regulatory informality challenge in Colombia:** Multiple sources (L2b Aviation Latin America Panel 2024; Saint Augustines University, 2026) note that Colombia faces significant challenges with drone operator informality — operators who are unaware of or disregard existing regulations. This limits the effectiveness of even a well-designed regulatory framework.

## 7. Recommendations from ICAO and JARUS on Medical Standards for RPAS Operators

### ICAO Recommendations

ICAO has not yet adopted binding SARPs specifically addressing medical standards for small/sUAS remote pilots operating in the Open or Specific categories. ICAO's position, reflected in Doc 10019 and the UAS Toolkit (https://www.icao.int/UA/UASToolkit), is that:

1. Remote pilots operating RPAS in non-segregated airspace should hold a "remote pilot licence" whose requirements, including medical standards, should be proportionate to the risk of the operation.
2. ICAO Annex 1 (Personnel Licensing) provisions are the primary reference, but "States should assess the unique nature of the RPS environment when determining medical fitness requirements."
3. For larger RPAS operating in controlled airspace under IFR (the Doc 10019 scope), a comprehensive medical certificate approach is expected; for sUAS/smaller RPAS, flexibility is the expected approach.
4. ICAO's Model UAS Regulations (Parts 101 and 102, June 2020) do not prescribe specific medical standards for Category A/B operators, focusing instead on self-attestation of fitness consistent with the JAR Doc 11 approach.

### JARUS Recommendations

JARUS provides the most granular international guidance on this topic:

- **Category A (Open)/Category B (Specific):** No formal medical certificate required. Operators must be in a physical and mental condition not to endanger safe operations. Category B requires a **self-declaration of medical fitness** to the UAS operator (based on SORA), not a formal physician examination (JAR Doc 11, Article 5).
- **Category C (Certified):** Formal **Medical Certificate Class 3** recommended (JARUS-FCL Recommendation, JAR Doc 03, Article 4.5.1), with the flexibility to use lower ICAO Annex 1 standards given the RPS environment.
- **Key flexibility principle:** "The competent authority may consider using the flexibility provided by ICAO Annex 1 regarding the medical assessment given the particular environment of the RPS and the conduct of RPAS operations" — meaning even for certified RPAS, the Class 3 standard may be further modified.

**Summary of ICAO/JARUS recommended medical standard spectrum:**

| Risk Category | ICAO/JARUS Recommendation | Colombia RAC 100 | FAA Part 107 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Category A (Open) | None / basic self-assessment | None (open category) | None (§107.17 self-cert) |
| Category B (Specific) | Self-declaration of fitness | **Annual occupational medical certificate** ✓ (exceeds recommendation) | Self-certification only (below recommendation) |
| Category C (Certified) | Medical Certificate Class 3 (ICAO Annex 1 flexibility) | Not yet implemented | Not yet applicable |

Colombia's mandatory occupational medical certificate for specific category operations **exceeds the JARUS Category B recommendation** (which requires only self-declaration) but **does not reach** the JARUS Category C recommendation (Medical Certificate Class 3). This places Colombia in a unique intermediate position: more demanding than international guidance for commercial operators, but not implementing the full aviation medical standard.

The FAA, by contrast, applies a standard below JARUS recommendations even for commercial operations — justified by the low-risk characteristics of small UAS operations and the operational constraints of Part 107.

## 8. Conclusion

The comparison of Colombian RAC 100 and FAA 14 CFR Part 107 reveals two mature but philosophically distinct regulatory approaches to sUAS/RPAS governance, shaped by different risk tolerances, institutional frameworks, and degrees of ICAO/JARUS alignment.

**Colombia's RAC 100** represents a risk-stratified, pre-authorization system with substantively higher operator and pilot certification requirements for commercial operations. The mandatory annual occupational medical certificate — while falling short of ICAO Annex 1 aviation medical standards — is the single most distinctive difference from FAA Part 107. Combined with mandatory practical flight training (a worldwide regulatory rarity), mandatory insurance, per-operation authorization requirements, and a formal operator certification regime, RAC 100 creates a more demanding compliance environment for commercial UAS operators than Part 107.

**FAA Part 107** represents a risk-proportionate self-governance model with maximum operational flexibility for certified remote pilots, enabled by the LAANC automated airspace authorization system and an accessible online certification pathway with no practical flight test. The absence of any medical examination requirement is grounded in sound theoretical risk analysis (limited harm consequence of small UAS incapacitation relative to manned aviation) and has functioned without documented adverse incidents attributable to medical gaps since 2016. The recent additions of Remote ID (2024) and Operations Over People categories represent the most significant updates to the Part 107 framework and provide safety architecture that RAC 100 currently lacks in the Remote ID domain.

**ICAO and JARUS** provide a flexible international framework that neither jurisdiction fully implements: JARUS Category B recommends self-declaration (FAA model) while JARUS Category C recommends a formal Class 3 medical certificate. Colombia occupies the policy space between these two recommendations with its occupational medical certificate requirement.

For researchers, policy analysts, and aviation medical examiners, the central policy question emerging from this comparison is: **Is any form of objective medical assessment for commercial UAS operators necessary and proportionate?** The evidence base is still developing. The 2007 Volpe/DOT study recommended second-class medical certification; JARUS Category B guidance recommends only self-declaration; Colombia's pragmatic occupational certificate model may offer a template for a risk-proportionate global minimum standard that provides objective safety assurance without the cost and access barriers of full aviation medical certification.

As drone operations grow in complexity — particularly BVLOS, night operations, cargo delivery, and swarm operations — the question of whether the current self-certification approaches (FAA, EASA) remain sufficient will merit ongoing regulatory review. Colombia's regulatory experience with the occupational medical certificate, once sufficient operational data accumulates, may provide an evidence base for evaluating this policy option internationally.
